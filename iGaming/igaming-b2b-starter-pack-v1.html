B2B map

First understand who sells what to whom.

Operator — buys technology, content, payments and services to run a player-facing brand.

Game Provider — supplies gaming content and related promotional mechanics.

Aggregator — distributes many providers through one integration layer.

Platform / PAM — supplies core account, wallet, bonus and back-office infrastructure.

PSP / Payment — supplies transaction rails and local payment coverage.

CRM / Data / SaaS — supplies engagement, analytics, automation, fraud or operational tooling.

02 / 20

How a B2B deal starts

Most deals begin with a business problem, not a product demo.

Market need — operator wants a market, product or capability.

Discovery — understand brands, markets, licences, traffic, stack and priorities.

Fit — confirm product, technical and commercial compatibility.

Demo — show only the workflows relevant to the buyer.

Proposal — scope, commercials, responsibilities and assumptions.

Decision — business, technical, compliance and legal stakeholders align.

Bad B2B question: “Want a demo?”

Better sequence: What markets do you operate? What are you trying to improve? What stack do you use? What is missing today? Then decide whether a demo is useful.

03 / 20

Know your customer

In B2B, 'the client' is usually several people with different goals.

Owner / C-level — commercial impact, speed, risk and strategic fit.

Product — content quality, roadmap, player experience and differentiation.

Marketing — campaigns, visibility, assets and promotional mechanics.

Operations — configuration, reporting, support and daily workflow.

Tech — API, integration, environments, reliability and maintenance.

Finance / Legal / Compliance — pricing, settlement, contract and regulatory fit.

04 / 20

Qualification

Not every lead deserves the same amount of time.

Market — where does the operator actually operate?

Licence — can the product legally be supplied there?

Product fit — does the buyer need what you sell?

Technical fit — can both stacks integrate?

Commercial fit — is there realistic volume / value?

Timing — is this an active project or only information gathering?

05 / 20

Commercial models

Know what creates revenue and what creates cost.

Revenue share — supplier receives an agreed percentage of a defined revenue base.

Fixed fee — recurring or one-off charge independent of gaming result.

Setup / integration fee — cost attached to onboarding or technical work.

Minimum guarantee — minimum commercial commitment under agreed terms.

Tiered pricing — economics change when volume crosses defined bands.

Custom deal — hybrids, bundles and market-specific structures are common.

06 / 20

Contracts

The sales deck gets attention; the contract defines reality.

Commercial definition — exactly what the percentage or fee applies to.

Territory — approved markets, brands, domains or entities.

Term / renewal — duration, renewal and termination mechanics.

Payment terms — invoice, currency, settlement and due dates.

SLA — service expectations and support responsibilities.

Liability / compliance — responsibilities, restrictions and required controls.

07 / 20

Integration lifecycle

Signed does not mean live.

Kickoff — owners, scope, markets and target launch.

Documentation — API, credentials, environments and configuration.

Development — both sides build required connections.

Testing / UAT — validate flows, errors, wallet, reporting and edge cases.

Certification — complete required product / compliance checks.

Production — deploy, smoke-test and monitor the live environment.

Signed→Kickoff→Develop→UAT→Certify→Go Live→Grow
08 / 20

Game provider operations

A provider relationship continues long after integration.

Game catalogue — titles, IDs, categories and availability.

RTP / configuration — approved configurations and operator setup.

Currency — supported currencies and denomination behaviour.

Launch calendar — new releases and market availability.

Maintenance — incidents, planned downtime and version changes.

Reporting — turnover, GGR, players, games, markets and operator performance.

09 / 20

Aggregator operations

Aggregation simplifies distribution but adds another relationship layer.

Provider onboarding — aggregator connects and maintains supplier integrations.

Operator integration — operator connects once to access multiple suppliers.

Content mapping — game IDs, categories, metadata and assets.

Commercial routing — contract structure determines who bills whom.

Incident routing — support may pass through aggregator and provider.

Market availability — content access still depends on supplier and jurisdiction rules.

10 / 20

Promotions B2B

Promotions are often a negotiation between supplier goals and operator goals.

Free rounds / spins — provider or operator-funded promotional rounds.

Tournament — ranking competition using qualifying game activity.

Cash / prize drop — rewards triggered under campaign rules.

1+1 / matching mechanics — campaign structure varies by supplier.

Leaderboard — points or qualifying behaviour drives ranking.

Budget pool — define funding, cap, eligible games, markets and settlement.

Operator asks: “What can you sponsor?”

Before answering, define market, brand, dates, eligible games, mechanic, budget cap, funding owner, assets, reporting and what success means. Otherwise “free promotion” becomes an operational problem later.

11 / 20

Campaign workflow

A good promotion fails easily when ownership is vague.

Objective — launch, exposure, turnover, retention or reactivation?

Eligibility — brand, market, player, game and date rules.

Funding — who pays and what is the cap?

Assets — banners, copy, game links and localisation.

Tracking — define success metrics before launch.

Reconciliation — confirm winners, cost, results and invoicing afterwards.

12 / 20

Account management

B2B retention is operational, not only relational.

Regular check-in — priorities, issues and upcoming launches.

Performance review — volume, growth, game mix and campaign results.

Opportunity mapping — new brands, markets, products and integrations.

Escalation — coordinate technical and operational incidents.

Roadmap — connect supplier releases with operator needs.

Documentation — keep decisions, owners and next actions visible.

13 / 20

Reporting & KPIs

Different B2B products need different scorecards.

Revenue — supplier revenue under the agreed commercial model.

Turnover / GGR — underlying operator activity where relevant.

Active players — reach and engagement of supplied content.

Game / product mix — which products actually create volume.

Operator growth — movement by brand, market or account.

Campaign lift — compare agreed campaign metrics with a meaningful baseline.

14 / 20

Pipeline

Sales pipeline is a sequence of evidence, not optimistic labels.

Lead — identifiable potential customer.

Qualified — fit and opportunity are credible enough to pursue.

Discovery / Demo — needs and product fit are being validated.

Commercial — pricing / deal structure is under discussion.

Contract / Integration — legal or technical execution is underway.

Live / Expansion — production account moves into growth and retention.

15 / 20

Demo strategy

Do not give the same demo to everyone.

Executive — outcome, differentiation, economics and implementation risk.

Product — catalogue, UX, configuration and roadmap.

Marketing — campaign tools, assets and promotion workflow.

Operations — back office, reports, controls and support workflow.

Technical — architecture, API, errors, environments and monitoring.

Finance — commercial logic, reconciliation and settlement visibility.

16 / 20

Technical literacy

You do not need to be an engineer, but you must speak enough technical language.

API — structured way systems request and exchange data.

Webhook / callback — event sent from one system to another.

API key / credential — secret used to authenticate a system.

Sandbox / staging — non-production environment for testing.

Production — live environment serving real users / transactions.

Logs / error codes — evidence used to diagnose integration problems.

17 / 20

Incident management

How you behave when something breaks is part of the product.

Confirm scope — one game, provider, brand, market or everyone?

Collect evidence — timestamp, ID, request, response and screenshots where useful.

Set severity — business impact determines urgency.

Route owner — operator, aggregator, provider, platform or payment team.

Communicate — status, impact and next update without guessing.

Postmortem — root cause, fix and prevention after resolution.

Never guess during an incident.

Separate confirmed facts, current impact, investigation status and next update. Fast communication is useful; confident speculation is not.

18 / 20

Expansion

A live client is the beginning of the account, not the end.

More content — increase catalogue / product adoption.

More brands — expand within the same group.

More markets — enable approved jurisdictions and currencies.

More campaigns — turn integration into player-facing activity.

Better placement — improve visibility based on evidence.

Cross-sell — introduce other relevant products only when there is fit.

19 / 20

Common mistakes

These mistakes make B2B teams look busy without creating much value.

Pitching before discovery.

Calling every logo a qualified lead.

Promising technical capability before confirming it.

Launching promotions without tracking or funding rules.

Treating signed contract as completed revenue.

Only contacting clients when you want something.

The B2B rule

Your job is not to know every answer immediately. Your job is to understand the question, know who owns the answer, verify it, communicate it clearly, and move the account forward without creating unnecessary risk.
